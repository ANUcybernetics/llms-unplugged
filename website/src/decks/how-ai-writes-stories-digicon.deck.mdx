# Scaling up

![bg](./assets/bg-information-flow.avif)

```notes
What they just did by hand is the same core loop an LLM runs, at human scale: look at the context, weigh every possible next word, roll the dice, write it down, repeat.

The next two slides cover how frontier models turn up three scale dials, and how post-training changes the kind of text they train on. Point people to the website lessons for why a table of counts can't be scaled and how transformers share weights instead. ~3 min.
```

## Now turn everything up

<ModelScaleBars
  startLabel="by hand"
  contextFrom={1}
  contextFromLabel="1 word"
  trainingFrom={84489}
  trainingFromLabel="one novel"
  modelFrom={49322529}
  modelFromLabel="49 million cells"
  footer="one axis, in powers of ten · three independent dials"
/>

```notes
Ask first: hands up if you've told a chatbot not to do something and it did it anyway. Take two answers. In a frontier model your instruction stays inside the context window the whole time, where it competes as one more stretch of text against everything else in the window. What the model is looking at right now shapes what comes next, just as in their model.

Then walk through the three dials. The shared axis is in powers of ten (thousand, million, billion, trillion), so each bar's length shows how many orders of magnitude it jumps:
- Context: their model looked at one word; Claude looks at up to a million tokens, enough for a whole conversation and more.
- Training text: one novel (about 84,000 words) versus forty-five trillion tokens, the training run Thinking Machines published for its open-weights Inkling model in July 2026 (about 530 million copies of the novel, or roughly 17 million years at the pace they just worked).
- Model size: a table of counts for the whole novel, with a row and a column for every word, has 49 million cells, while Claude Fable 5 is estimated at about five trillion parameters (Anthropic doesn't publish the figure).

Why can't you just scale up the table? The novel's table has a count in only 0.08% of its cells (one in 1,200), and reading more text makes it sparser because every new word adds an entire row and column. A trillion-cell table would be almost completely blank. A transformer shares its parameters instead: patterns are distributed across billions of weights, so a sentence about a cat helps predict a sentence about a dog, and the model can produce combinations that never appeared in its training data. Increasing context, data and parameter count improves performance predictably enough to plan large training runs around. Each dial has its own lesson on the website.
```

## “So is ChatGPT just counting?”

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-teachers-just-counting.avif)

the **same loop**: look at the words so far, weigh the next ones, pick one at
random

it looks at the whole conversation instead of one word, and learned from
trillions of words across the web

and the model is **one part of a bigger system**: tools it can call, filters on
what goes in and comes out, instructions you never see

```notes
Students always ask this. Every reply is still sampled one word at a time, which is why "regenerate" gives a different answer, just as two groups with the same book wrote different stories. Beyond the jump in scale (a whole conversation of context and trillions of words of training data), a chatbot wraps the model in ordinary software: hidden system instructions prepended before the user's message, tools the model can call (web search, a calculator, code execution), and input/output filters. Their paper sheets represent the model at the centre of that system.
```

<div class="qa-compare">
  <span class="qa-prompt">"Explain the moon landing to a 6 year old in a few sentences."</span>
  <span class="qa-label">pre-trained</span>
  <span class="qa-a">Explain the theory of gravity to a 6 year old. Explain the theory of relativity to a 6 year old in a few sentences. Explain the big bang theory to a 6 year old. Explain evolution to a 6 year old.</span>
  <span class="qa-label">post-trained</span>
  <span class="qa-a">People went to the moon, and they took pictures of what they saw, and sent them back to the earth so we could all see them.</span>
</div>

same model, same next-word loop---**post-training** is a second round of
training on examples of instructions being followed

```notes
Beyond the three scale dials, post-training changes which text the model learns from.

Read the prompt out, then the first reply, and pause for the room to spot what happened. That's GPT-3 straight out of pre-training, continuing the text just like their model. On the internet, "explain X to a 6 year old" usually appears in lists of homework prompts and writing exercises, so the most likely continuation is another question in the list.

Then read the second reply. That's the same model after post-training (published by OpenAI in January 2022 as InstructGPT, the precursor to ChatGPT). OpenAI trained it further on examples of people writing helpful responses to instructions, then had human raters rank its attempts and reinforced the preferred ones. It still runs the same next-word sampling loop, but after training on instruction-response pairs, the likeliest continuation of an instruction is an answer.

If you have time, mention sycophancy: ask who has pushed back on a chatbot and watched it immediately fold, even when the user was wrong. Human raters comparing two replies tended to prefer the agreeable one, so agreement was reinforced during post-training, and the model has no separate check for whether a claim is true.
```

## Running it in your class

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-teachers-ledger-class.avif)

everything to print is in the **pack on the lesson page**: no books to find

**60 minutes**: one of the pack's pre-tallied picture books per group, counters,
a cup and a tub per group, and a whiteboard for training "the" together

**90 minutes**: add blank sheets and one of the pack's training texts per group,
then the class story

```notes
Step out of the student role here, cover the logistics briefly, and open for questions. Recommend using the lesson pack rather than bringing their own picture books: the generation sheets come pre-tallied, and the training texts are written so no word needs more than four boxes. Teachers who want to use a book of their own can generate sheets from any text on the website's tools page.

The snags you saw today are the ones their class will hit: counters left in the cup from the last draw, a word with no row (start again from any word), and in the 90-minute version two people starting a row for the same word. The whiteboard tally in the 60-minute version is a good job for a student. With more than five groups the books repeat; spread the repeats around the room.
```
