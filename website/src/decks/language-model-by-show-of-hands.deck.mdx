## the algorithm

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-algorithm-generation.avif)

1. I **call out** the last token we wrote (and its colour)
2. **hands up** if it's the _boxed_ token on your page
3. I **pick a hand** --- choose a matching pair and read its next token
4. I **write it down**, and we go again

```notes
describe the algorithm (reading from the screen is fine)

then call for a volunteer and get the first pair: boxed token first, both go on the board

Sampling caveat: one hand per person approximates sampling occurrences. Someone with two matches has the same chance of being selected as someone with one; even choosing uniformly among their pairs underweights each occurrence. Keep the activity simple and acknowledge this if asked.
```

## what role did you play?

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-core-insight-portrait.avif)

the pairs on your page → the model's **counts**

the matching pairs → possible **continuations**

choosing a continuation → **sampling**

the sentence I wrote → **generation**

```notes
read from the slide

about 6k observed token pairs, stored explicitly. Neural model weights are learned numerical parameters, not a list of observed pairs. We'll compare them after the reveal.
```

## what carries over to LLMs?

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-algorithm-generation.avif)

our model: **explicit counts**, one token of context

neural models: **learned representations**, much longer context

both: generate successive tokens from **conditional distributions**

```notes
Recent n-gram research asks which neural-model predictions corpus statistics capture, and where they diverge. Our room makes the counts inspectable; it does not reproduce a transformer's internal computation.

[Nguyen (NeurIPS 2024)](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/b1c446eebd9a317dd0e96b16908c821a-Abstract-Conference.html) compares n-gram rulesets with transformer predictions: 79% top-token agreement on TinyStories, 68% on Wikipedia in the studied models. Agreement on one token does not mean identical distributions.

[Svete and Cotterell (NAACL 2024)](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.381/) show transformers with hard or sparse attention can represent any n-gram model. This is a capacity result, not a claim about how trained models work.

[Infini-gram (COLM 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17377) scales counts to five trillion tokens and unbounded context lengths, using them to analyse and improve neural models; [OLMoTrace (ACL 2025)](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-demo.18/) builds on it to trace a model's output back to verbatim spans of its training data. Choose one example to explain aloud; the links let educators follow all four afterwards.
```

would **more copies** make our model more capable?

![bg](./assets/bg-hall-generation-wide.avif)

```notes
Give the room time to answer. More complete copies repeat the same evidence and leave the underlying count proportions unchanged. More people holding identical sheets do not add context or knowledge.

Then ask why locally plausible fragments can lose coherence as the sequence grows. Our model only conditions on the last token; it cannot use an earlier topic or subject to choose the next continuation. Longer context changes what predictions can depend on.

Use these answers as an informal check: can participants explain what our model does, and identify a limit of transferring that explanation to neural LLMs?
```
