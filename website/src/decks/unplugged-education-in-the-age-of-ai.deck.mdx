## everything it knows came from two books

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-many-texts.avif)

_The Cat in the Hat_ and Hemingway's _Cat in the Rain_

**3,278** tokens · **496** distinct · **3,276** token pairs dealt round the room

nothing **outside the books** can come out

```notes
Dr Seuss, plus a 1925 Hemingway short story about an American couple in an Italian hotel and a cat out in the rain. Two texts joined by one shared noun: `cat`.

Three properties of language models are much easier to see here than in Claude. First, training data. Every one of the 3,276 pairs in those two texts is on a single sheet in this room. For the last five minutes the model existed across the room as a whole; anyone who stayed home took a piece of it with them.

And the model can only recombine what it was given. Ask about Melbourne and there's no `Melbourne` row in the room. Swap the texts and the same sheets, hands and coin tosses produce completely different sentences. Frontier models work the same way: what they can say comes from what they read, and someone chose what they read.
```

## someone chose all of this

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-design-your-model.avif)

the **books**: Seuss and Hemingway

the **context**: one word back

the **tokens**: a full stop counts as a word

the **sampling rule**: pick any hand at random

```notes
Third, design choices. I made every decision on this slide before you walked in.

I chose the two texts. I set the context window to one word back, which is why our sentence lost its thread. I made punctuation a token, so full stops got called and picked like words.

And I chose the sampling rule: pick a hand at random. If I'd picked the fastest hand instead, we'd have sampled the quickest readers in the front rows without changing a word of the data.

Commercial models are built out of the same kinds of decisions: what goes into the training set, how text is tokenised, how much randomness is used at generation time, what the model is trained to refuse. When someone says "the AI decided", ask who chose the books and the rules.
```

```notes
Three independent dials separate our room from a frontier LLM. First, context window: how much text the model sees before each guess. We looked one token back; Claude looks back up to a million tokens.
```

## what it took

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-insight-tool-use.avif)

**Claude Fable 5.2**, before its public launch

**204** worlds in the first twenty hours, **471** by launch day

a lead agent and **28 teams**: about **25,000** agent runs

a rule book: **204 sections, 87,500 words**

```notes
The 25,000 is Claude's own estimate, and most of those runs did a single small job, like judging one screenshot. By day ten there was a lead agent that owned the engine and shipped every release, and 28 teams building worlds in parallel.

The rule book is the bit to tell. Mollick asked the agents to "polish", and they decided every new version had to beat the old one in a blind vote by judge agents. Rules piled up as problems came up and nothing was ever cut: 84 sections by the next morning, 204 sections and 87,500 words after twelve days. Shipping a world took a day and reading the rule book ate much of an agent's context window, so output fell from hundreds of worlds a day to about a dozen. A separate team of agents looking at the process from outside got it back to thirty a day.

This is the harness, the tools and the loop from before, run at scale: agents calling agents, and checks deciding what ships.
```

## Claude isn't just a model any more

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-tool-use-operator.avif)

the **model**: words in, words out---that was you

the **harness**: runs the loop, calls tools, asks permission---that was me

the **tools**: files, the web, a terminal, **your inbox**

```notes
What people call Claude in 2026 is a model wrapped in a harness with tools. The model emits tokens formatted as a tool request; the harness intercepts that request, runs the tool, pastes the output back into the context window, and lets the model continue generating. Simon Willison's summary: an agent is a model running tools in a loop to achieve a goal.

You already watched a harness during our first run: I called the context word, picked the hand and wrote the token on the board. Now let's give our harness a tool.
```

## the room becomes an agent

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-tool-use-triggers.avif)

we carry on from our sentence, same as before

whenever the room's word has an **`a`** in it, that's a **tool call**

the tool: the **second word** of the subject of the **third email** in your
inbox

first hand up gets it---and gets **a veto**

```notes
When the token we pick contains the letter `a` (`cat`, `hat`, `that`, `and`, roughly one word in four here), we trigger a tool call.

The tool reads the second word of the subject line of the third email in your inbox ("Re:" counts as word one). Take the first hand with a word ready, and before they speak, ask: "Happy to share that?" They can decline --- that's the permission prompt.

Write the inbox word on the board after the `a` word, then call the `a` word again to continue. Because our paper sheets have no entries for arbitrary words from someone's email, the bigram model has to resume from the last token it knows.
```

## what role did you play this time?

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-tool-use-handoff.avif)

a word with an `a` → a **tool call**

your inbox → the **tool**

the word you read out → the **tool result**

"happy to share that?" → the **permission prompt**

me, coordinating all of that → the **harness**

```notes
Production agents use the same five pieces, with more tools and fewer permission prompts.

Notice one difference: our bigram model had to ignore the inbox word and continue from the token before it, whereas a real LLM feeds the email text straight into its context window. Anyone who sends you an email can put text into your agent's context, including text written like instructions. That's prompt injection, and after this exercise a class can see why it happens.
```

## unplugged in your classroom

```notes
Most commercial chatbots prohibit under-13s or under-18s in their terms of service; paper has no logins or age gates.
```
