## what we get told about AI

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-dialogue.avif)

it **understands** you---it's basically a mind

it's **just autocomplete**---it's basically a parlour trick

```notes
Both of these are on offer everywhere, usually from people who are confident. And notice they're opposites that do the same job: each one tells you there's nothing in the middle worth your attention. Either it's beyond you, or it's beneath you. Either way, don't look inside.

Say plainly where you stand, because the rest of the talk could be misheard: I'm not here to tell you it's a parlour trick. The real ones are extraordinary, and I use them every day. That's exactly why it's worth seeing inside.

The real answer sits between the two stories, and the heart of it fits in a room like this one.
```

## unplugged

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-paper-mechanism.avif)

computer science **without the computer**

the **idea**, not the device: binary on cards, sorting networks in chalk on the
playground

CS Unplugged: Tim Bell, Ian Witten and Mike Fellows, since the 1990s

```notes
Some of you will have run these; hands up if you have. For everyone else: CS Unplugged has been teaching computer science without computers for about thirty years. Five cards with dots on them, and a class counts in binary. A sorting network chalked on the playground, and the class sorts itself by walking it.

It works because the device hides the idea. On a screen, the interesting part happens where you can't see it. On paper, with bodies, it happens slowly and in front of you, and nobody's waiting for a login.

My claim is that this matters more now than when it started. The less you can see inside a technology, the more it's worth taking it apart by hand. And there has never been a technology people can see less of than this one.
```

## everything it knows came from two books

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-many-texts.avif)

**3,278** tokens · **496** distinct · **3,276** token pairs dealt round the room

no single sheet has the whole picture

nothing **outside the books** can come out

```notes
First, training data. Every one of the 3,276 pairs in the two books is on exactly one sheet in each set of fifty. The model wasn't in my laptop and it wasn't on anyone's sheet --- for those few minutes it existed only across the whole room. If somebody had stayed home, part of it would have been missing.

And it can only recombine what it was given. Ask it about Melbourne and there's no `Melbourne` row anywhere in the room. Swap the books and you'd have a different model with exactly the same machinery: same sheets, same hands, same coin tosses, different sentences.

A frontier model is the same in this respect. What it can say comes from what it read, and what it read was a choice.
```

## what the room says about `she` and `he`

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-distribution.avif)

`she` → said ×8, **liked ×6**, went, opened, studied, smiled, sat, stood, held,
talked...

`he` → said ×6, was ×4, should ×3, **fell ×2**, lit, shut, put, picked, ran,
got...

```notes
These are every continuation in the room. Ask this model about `she` and you get Hemingway's wife, who spends the story at a window, liking things and wanting a cat: she liked, she smiled, she studied, she stood. Ask about `he` and you mostly get the Cat in the Hat, who knocks things over: he fell, he lit, he shut, he picked, he ran. Six `she liked` in the room, and not one `he liked`.

That's not "women like things and men do things". It's two books, one from 1925. But the model can't tell the difference between what these books say and what the world is like. It has nothing else to go on.

Real models read a large part of the internet, and the internet has its own `she` and `he`. Nobody put the bias in on purpose. It came in with the books. The labs put serious work into finding and correcting it, and it's hard work for exactly the reason you've just seen: it isn't a bug in the machinery, it's in the data.
```

## someone chose all of this

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-design-your-model.avif)

the **books**: Seuss and Hemingway, not your school library

the **context**: one word back, not ten

the **tokens**: a full stop counts as a word

the **pick**: a random hand, not the fastest

```notes
Third, design decisions. Every line here was a choice, and I made all of them before you walked in.

I chose the books. I chose to look one word back, which is why the sentence wandered. I chose to make punctuation a token, which is why full stops were out there getting picked like everything else.

And the last one you lived through: I picked a hand at random. If I'd taken the fastest hand, the model would say whatever the quickest readers in the room had on their sheets. Same sheets, different sentences, and nobody touched the data.

Real models are made of decisions like these, made by people at companies: what to read, how to cut it into tokens, how much randomness to allow, what to train it to refuse. When someone says "the AI decided", it's worth asking who chose the books.
```

## three things Claude\* does that we didn't

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-attention.avif)

it looks at **more than one word** of context

it can say what it **never saw**---`cat` teaches it about `dog`

it's been taught to **answer**, not just continue

```notes
The asterisk is yours to explain, in one line: Claude here means any frontier model --- ChatGPT, Gemini, Llama. Nothing on this slide is specific to Anthropic's; I just need a name to point at.

Say each in a sentence, resist explaining any of them; each one has a full module on the website and none of them fits in this talk.

One word of context is why our sentence wandered --- when I called `the`, nobody could tell whether we'd just written "sat in the" or "hit the". Claude looks back over the whole conversation.

Our model rates any pair it never saw as flatly impossible, however sensible. A real model spreads what it learns across billions of numbers instead of separate cells, so what it learns about `cat` reaches `dog`.

And ours only ever continues text. Ask it a question and it continues the question --- that's not it refusing, answering just isn't a thing it does. Turning that into something that answers you is another round of training, on conversations.

None of these changed the loop. Look at the context, weigh the options, pick one, write it down.
```

```notes
Three independent scales grow. The first is how much the model looks at before each guess. Ours looked at exactly one token --- which is why our sentence wandered --- and Claude looks back over up to a million tokens, the whole conversation and then some.

The second is how much text went past the counter. Ours read 3,278 tokens; fifteen trillion is the published figure for a recent open model's training run.
```

## Claude isn't just a model any more

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-tool-use-operator.avif)

the **model**: words in, words out---that was you

the **harness**: runs the loop, calls tools, asks permission---that was me

the **tools**: files, the web, a terminal, **your inbox**

```notes
In 2026 the thing people call Claude is mostly the second and third lines. The model writes something that looks like a request. The software around it --- the harness --- notices, runs the tool, pastes the result back into the text, and the model carries on writing. Simon Willison's definition is the best one going: an agent is a model that runs tools in a loop to achieve a goal.

You've already seen a harness at work. During the run I called out the word, picked the hand and wrote the result down. The model never did any of that. I did.

So let's give our model a tool.
```

## the room becomes an agent

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-tool-use-triggers.avif)

we carry on from our sentence, same as before

when someone reads out `.` `?` or `!`, that's a **tool call**

the tool: the **second word** of the subject of the **third email** in your
inbox

first hand up gets it---and gets **a veto**

```notes
Phones out, just for this round. That's the one tool in this talk that isn't paper.

Walk it once. When an end-of-sentence token comes up, stop. Ask the room for the tool: second word of the subject of the third email in your inbox. A reply's "Re:" counts as the first word, so it's usually the real one. Take the first hand that has a word ready, and before they read it out, ask: "Happy to share that?" They can say no. If they do, say so out loud: that was the permission prompt.

Write the word they give you, then the full stop, and carry on from the full stop. Our model has no row for whatever came back from someone's inbox, so it has to pick up from the punctuation.
```

## what role did you play this time?

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-tool-use-handoff.avif)

the full stop → a **tool call**

your inbox → the **tool**

the word you read out → the **tool result**

"happy to share that?" → the **permission prompt**

me, deciding all of that → the **harness**

```notes
Every agent you'll read about is this, with a lot more tools and a lot less asking.

One more thing worth thirty seconds. Our model ignored the word from the inbox: it carried on from the full stop. Claude doesn't. The email's text goes into what it reads next. So anyone who can send you an email can put words in front of your agent --- including words that look like instructions. That has a name, prompt injection, and it's one of the big open problems for everyone building agents. Your students can now say what it is and why it happens.
```

## what they had to learn

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-chain.avif)

the **context**: what the agent can see, and what it can't

the **tools**: what it may run, and when it has to ask

the **loop**: tests and checks that tell it when it's wrong

```notes
And here's the part that matters for this room. The course doesn't teach tricks for a particular tool; one of its learning outcomes is to explain how these agents work. To use them well, students had to understand what kind of thing they were working with.

What it can see: the context window, the same idea as our one word of context, scaled up. What it may do: tools and permissions, the round we just ran. And the loop: the agent writes, a test runs, the result goes back into the text, it tries again. That's the harness, and building a good one is a big part of the course.

Every one of those you've just acted out on paper. The concepts my university students needed to work with agents are the ones your students can act out in a lesson.
```

the question has moved

from what it **says**

to what it's **allowed to do**

```notes
With a chatbot, the worry is what it says. With an agent, the worry is what it does: which tools it gets, what it may do without asking, when a person has to say yes.

Those aren't facts about the model. They're settings someone chose. That's exactly where Bandura's "influence" stops being abstract: a school can decide what an agent may touch, a teacher can decide what it may do in their class, and a student can decide what they'll let it read.
```

## unplugged in your classroom

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-teachers-class.avif)

no devices, no accounts, no age limits

students pick the **text**, and see their own bias come back

the tools are **students with a job**: the third book on the shelf, the clock,
the window

and someone has to be the **harness**

```notes
The practical side, quickly.

No devices, no accounts, no age limits. Most of the big chatbots are 13-plus or 18-plus by their own terms; a sheet of paper isn't.

Let students choose the text. Deal sheets from their own writing, or a book they're studying, and ask what the model says about `she` and `he`. Bias stops being a lecture topic when it comes out of their own text.

Phones are the wrong tool in a classroom, so the agent round needs a different one. The tools become students with a job: the second word of the title of the third book on the shelf, the time on the clock, what's outside the window. And one student is the harness, deciding what goes in and when to ask. That role starts the best discussion: what would you let it do without asking you first?

If you're in workshop 2A, that's where you build your own model, by hand, from scratch.
```

it doesn't make you an expert

it makes you **harder to bullsh\*t**

```notes
Be careful not to oversell. You can't build Claude now, and nothing here tells you whether it should be marking your students' essays.

And it cuts both ways, back to the two stories at the start. You're harder to sell "it's a mind", and just as hard to sell "it's only autocomplete". Neither survives having run one.
```
