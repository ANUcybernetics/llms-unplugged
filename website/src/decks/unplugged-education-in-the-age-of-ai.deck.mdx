## unplugged

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-paper-mechanism.avif)

computer science **without the computer**

the **idea**, not the device: binary on cards, sorting networks in chalk on the
playground

CS Unplugged: Tim Bell, Ian Witten and Mike Fellows, since the 1990s

```notes
For everyone who didn't put a hand up: CS Unplugged has been teaching computer science without computers for about thirty years. Five cards with dots on them, and a class counts in binary. A sorting network chalked on the playground, and the class sorts itself by walking it.

It works because the device hides the idea. On a screen, the interesting part happens where you can't see it. On paper, with bodies, it happens slowly and in front of you, and nobody's waiting for a login.

My claim is that this matters more now than when it started. The less you can see inside a technology, the more it's worth taking it apart by hand. And there has never been a technology people can see less of than this one.
```

## everything it knows came from two books

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-many-texts.avif)

_The Cat in the Hat_ and Hemingway's _Cat in the Rain_

**3,278** tokens · **496** distinct · **3,276** token pairs dealt round the room

nothing **outside the books** can come out

```notes
The reveal: Dr Seuss, and a Hemingway short story from 1925 that most people haven't read, about an American couple in a hotel in Italy and a cat sheltering from the rain. Two books, joined at one shared word: "cat".

Now that you've seen it, three things are easier to see here than they are with Claude. First, training data. Every one of the 3,276 pairs in the two books is on exactly one sheet. The model wasn't in my laptop and it wasn't on anyone's sheet --- for those few minutes it existed only across the whole room. If somebody had stayed home, part of it would have been missing.

And it can only recombine what it was given. Ask it about Melbourne and there's no `Melbourne` row anywhere in the room. Swap the books and you'd have a different model with exactly the same machinery: same sheets, same hands, same coin tosses, different sentences.

A frontier model is the same in this respect. What it can say comes from what it read, and what it read was a choice.
```

```notes
Real models read a large part of the internet, and the internet has its own `she` and `he`. Nobody put the bias in on purpose. It came in with the books. The labs put serious work into finding and correcting it, and it's hard work for exactly the reason you've just seen: it isn't a bug in the machinery, it's in the data.
```

## someone chose all of this

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-design-your-model.avif)

the **books**: Seuss and Hemingway, not your school library

the **context**: one word back, not ten

the **tokens**: a full stop counts as a word

the **rule for picking**: any hand at random, not the fastest

```notes
Third, design decisions. Every line here was a choice, and I made all of them before you walked in.

I chose the books. I chose to look one word back, which is why the sentence wandered. I chose to make punctuation a token, which is why full stops were out there getting picked like everything else.

And the last one you lived through. Each pick was random, but picking at random was my rule. If I'd taken the fastest hand, the model would say whatever the quickest readers in the room had on their sheets. Same sheets, different sentences, and nobody touched the data.

Real models are made of decisions like these, made by people at companies: what to read, how to cut it into tokens, how much randomness to allow, what to train it to refuse. When someone says "the AI decided", it's worth asking who chose the books.
```

## Claude isn't just a model any more

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-tool-use-operator.avif)

the **model**: words in, words out---that was you

the **harness**: runs the loop, calls tools, asks permission---that was me

the **tools**: files, the web, a terminal, **your inbox**

```notes
In 2026 the thing people call Claude is mostly the second and third lines. The model writes something that looks like a request. The software around it --- the harness --- notices, runs the tool, pastes the result back into the text, and the model carries on writing. Simon Willison's definition is the best one going: an agent is a model that runs tools in a loop to achieve a goal.

You've already seen a harness at work. During the run I called out the word, picked the hand and wrote the result down. The model never did any of that. I did.

So let's give our model a tool.
```

## the room becomes an agent

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-tool-use-triggers.avif)

we carry on from our sentence, same as before

whenever the room's word has an **`a`** in it, that's a **tool call**

the tool: the **second word** of the subject of the **third email** in your
inbox

first hand up gets it---and gets **a veto**

```notes
Walk it once. When the word that comes up has an `a` in it --- `cat`, `hat`, `that`, `and` --- stop: the model has just made a tool call. That's about one word in four in these books, so expect a call within a minute.

Ask the room for the tool: second word of the subject of the third email in your inbox. A reply's "Re:" counts as the first word, so it's usually the real one. Take the first hand that has a word ready, and before they read it out, ask: "Happy to share that?" They can say no. If they do, say so out loud: that was the permission prompt.

Write the inbox word down after the `a` word, then call out the `a` word again and carry on from it. Our model has no row for whatever came back from someone's inbox, so it has to pick up from the word before.
```

## what role did you play this time?

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-tool-use-handoff.avif)

a word with an `a` → a **tool call**

your inbox → the **tool**

the word you read out → the **tool result**

"happy to share that?" → the **permission prompt**

me, deciding all of that → the **harness**

```notes
Every agent you'll read about is this, with a lot more tools and a lot less asking.

One more thing worth thirty seconds. Our model ignored the word from the inbox: it carried on from the word before. Claude doesn't. The email's text goes into what it reads next. So anyone who can send you an email can put words in front of your agent --- including words that look like instructions. That has a name, prompt injection, and it's one of the big open problems for everyone building agents. Your students can now say what it is and why it happens.
```

## what they had to learn

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-chain.avif)

the **context**: what the agent can see, and what it can't

the **tools**: what it may run, and when it has to ask

the **loop**: tests and checks that tell it when it's wrong

```notes
And here's the part that matters for this room. The course doesn't teach tricks for a particular tool; one of its learning outcomes is to explain how these agents work. To use them well, students had to understand what kind of thing they were working with.

What it can see: the context window, the same idea as our one word of context, scaled up. What it may do: tools and permissions, the round we just ran. And the loop: the agent writes, a test runs, the result goes back into the text, it tries again. That's the harness, and building a good one is a big part of the course.

Every one of those you've just acted out on paper. The concepts my university students needed to work with agents are the ones your students can act out in a lesson.
```

the question has moved from what it **says** to what it's **allowed to do**

```notes
With a chatbot, the worry is what it says. With an agent, the worry is what it does: which tools it gets, what it may do without asking, when a person has to say yes.
```

## unplugged in your classroom

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-teachers-class.avif)

no devices, no accounts, no age limits

students pick the **text**, and see their own bias come back

the tools are **students with a job**: the third book on the shelf, the clock,
the window

and someone has to be the **harness**

```notes
The practical side, quickly.

No devices, no accounts, no age limits. Most of the big chatbots are 13-plus or 18-plus by their own terms; a sheet of paper isn't.

Let students choose the text, and ask what the model says about `she` and `he`. Bias stops being a lecture topic when it comes out of their own text.

Phones are the wrong tool in a classroom, so the agent round needs a different one. The tools become students with a job: the second word of the title of the third book on the shelf, the time on the clock, what's outside the window. And one student is the harness, deciding what goes in and when to ask. That role starts the best discussion: what would you let it do without asking you first?
```
