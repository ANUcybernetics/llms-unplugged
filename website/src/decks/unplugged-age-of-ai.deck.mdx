  light up the two clauses in turn, and the second is the one the whole talk
  is aimed at. It is repeated, already lit, on slide 21 to close the loop.

  The scale run (15-19) grows one axis only: how many words of context the
  table remembers, with the vocabulary held fixed. That is the axis that
  actually explodes, and vocabulary is not --- a tokeniser exists to keep it a
  bounded constant, and a real model spends only a sliver of itself on it. Do
  not reintroduce a vocabulary-size zoom; it makes the false claim that a
  bigger vocabulary is what makes a model big. Slides 18-19 are the pay-off:
  the table cannot be filled, so a real model isn't one.

## Does 'Unplugged' still matter in the age of AI?

![bg](./assets/bg-title-wide.avif)

> Everyday life is increasingly regulated by complex technologies that most
> people <span class="fragment quote-emph-minor">neither understand</span>
> <span class="fragment quote-emph">nor believe they can do much to
> influence</span>.
>
> <cite>Albert Bandura, 2001</cite>

{/* notes:
Tim had this on screen about fifteen minutes ago. I want to pick it up and point it at one technology in particular, because Bandura wrote it in 2001 and it has aged into a description of this year.

Read it out whole first, with nothing lit up. Then two clicks, because there are two failures in that sentence and they are not equal.

First click, `neither understand`: ordinary. I don't understand my car's transmission and I get by.

Second click, `nor believe they can do much to influence`: the expensive one. Having concluded, in advance, that this is not a thing you get a say in. That's the failure AI is currently manufacturing at scale, and it's the one this talk is aimed at.

## between us, we have the whole book

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-participants.avif)

**1,962** words · **240** distinct · **1,961** token pairs dealt round the room

no single sheet has the whole picture

{/* notes:
Every one of the 1,961 word-pairs in the book was dealt to exactly one person, and the deal deliberately spreads each context across as many people as it will go. So the model isn't in my laptop and it isn't on anyone's sheet --- it only exists across the whole room. If somebody had stayed home, part of it is missing and some contexts will draw no hands at all.

That's not a metaphor for how a model is distributed. For the next five minutes it's literally where the model is.
*/}

can I have a **volunteer**?

{/* notes:
Someone near the front, sheet in hand. Ask them to read out any one pair from their page --- both words, boxed one first.

Press `b` for the whiteboard and write both words up. That's the seed, and it matters that it came off a sheet rather than out of my head: the room supplies the first two words as well as every word after them.

Then say what happens next --- the second of those two words is the one I'm about to call out --- and move on. The algorithm slide lands better with a real word already on the board.

If their pair is an odd one (a full stop, a name that appears once), take it anyway --- a thin first round is a real property of the model, not a failure. It can't dead-end on you, so you never need a second volunteer for that reason.
*/}

## the algorithm

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-algorithm-generation.avif)

1. **call out** the last word we wrote (w/colour)
2. **hands up** if it appears as the _boxed_ word on your sheet
3. **pick a hand at random** and you read out the corresponding _next_ (non-boxed) word
4. **write it down**---and then repeat the process

{/* notes:
Walk them through it once slowly, against the two words already on the board, because the whole demo depends on step 2 and step 3.

Step 2: one hand each. If you've got more than one match, go with the first one you spot.

Step 3 is the one to labour, briefly. I pick at random --- not whoever shouts first, because that samples the fastest reader in the room rather than the book. Randomly is what makes the room behave like the model.

Don't explain why yet. They'll see it.
*/}

# let's run it

![bg](./assets/bg-div-lessons.avif)

<Timer minutes={5} overlay />

{/* notes:
Whiteboard is already up with the volunteer's two words. Call out the second of them and go. Five minutes on the timer, so keep the rounds moving --- no commentary between hands going up and a word landing on the board.

If you need to restart, the loud seeds are `the` (97 pairs out in the room across 30 different next words), then `and` (69), `I` (59), `you` (34), `we` (26). Avoid restarting from a full stop; it works, but the first thing the room does is argue about punctuation.

Two things to narrate while it runs, once each, without stopping the flow:

The hands are the distribution. Say `the` and a forest goes up; say a rare word and you get two. Nobody in this room has been taught weighted sampling and the room is doing it anyway --- the number of hands for a continuation is how often it happens in the book. (Pedant's footnote, only if a pedant asks: for contexts more common than the room is big, somebody holds two matches and raises one hand, which flattens the busiest contexts slightly.)

The choosing is a coin toss. When five hands are up, the four I don't pick were all real continuations of the book. Different pick, different sentence. This is the same reason the "regenerate" button gives you a different answer.

Hands will go up every round --- you can rely on that. Every distinct token in this book appears as a boxed word somewhere, so there is no word you can call that has nothing following it. (True of every corpus in the repo, not just this one.) So don't plan a "running dry" beat; there isn't one.

The one way the room goes quiet is an empty seat: that person's sheet went home with them, and the pairs on it are simply gone. If it happens, say exactly that --- it's the same point as slide 6, made for you --- and reseed.

Stop while they still want more, around 15 words. Read the sentence back.
*/}

## what role did you play?

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-core-insight-portrait.avif)

each word on your sheet → a **token**

your sheets → the **parameters**/**weights** of the **model**

the hands in the air → the **probability distribution**

the random pick → **sampling**

the sentence on the board → **generation**

{/* notes:
The jargon that gets used to make this stuff sound unreachable, and you've now got a physical referent for every bit of it. Go along the list at a fair clip --- the point is the mapping, not a definition of each term.

Token they've already met, on the sheet and back on slide 6; this is where it sticks, because everything else is measured in them. One unit of text --- for us one word or one punctuation mark, which is why the full stops are out there getting picked like everything else. (A real model chops rarer words into pieces, which is why it can spell out a word it has never seen. Say it only if it comes up.) Parameters and weights are the same thing under two names; you'll hear both.

Worth saying out loud: those are the real words. When a researcher says a model has 1.5 trillion parameters, they mean this, a very large pile of the thing on your lap.
*/}

## three things Claude\* does that we didn't

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-attention.avif)

it looks at **more than one word** of context

it can say what it **never saw**---`cat` teaches it about `dog`

it's been taught to **answer**, not just continue

{/* notes:
The asterisk is yours to explain, in one line: Claude here means any frontier model --- ChatGPT, Gemini, Llama. Nothing on this slide is specific to Anthropic's; I just need a name to point at.

Say each in a sentence, resist explaining any of them; each one has a full lesson on the website and none of them fits in this talk.

One word of context is why our sentence wandered --- when I called `the`, nobody could tell whether we'd just written "sat in the" or "hit the". Claude looks back over the whole conversation.

Our model rates any pair it never saw as flatly impossible, however sensible. A real model spreads what it learns across billions of numbers instead of separate cells, so what it learns about `cat` reaches `dog`.

And ours only ever continues text. Ask it a question and it continues the question --- that's not it refusing, answering just isn't a thing it does. Turning that into something that answers you is another round of training, on conversations.

None of these changed the loop. Look at the context, weigh the options, pick one, write it down.
*/}

{/* notes:
Make it personal before you make it big. Ask the room: who can tell me what comes after `in the`? Nobody can --- and not because they're slow. Every sheet out there is filed under *one* boxed word, so a two-word question isn't a harder version of what they did for five minutes, it's a different set of sheets that nobody here is holding.

Same book. Same 240 words. The only thing that changed is that we want to remember *two* words instead of one --- which would have fixed the wandering, because "sat in the" and "hit the" would no longer be the same question.

And look what it costs. The grid needs a row for every context, so asking for one more word of memory multiplies the whole thing by the size of the vocabulary again. This stage becomes a couple of suburban blocks.

Three words would be a third of the Acton campus. For a picture book. Say that one, don't draw it.
*/}

<GridZoom cells={1e20} label="a real model, looking back three words" detail="100,000⁴ = 100 quintillion cells" compareCells={15e12} compareLabel="everything it ever read" note="20 × the surface of the Earth" />

{/* notes:
Last step the picture survives, so read it rather than draw it. A real vocabulary is about 100,000 tokens --- and that's a deliberate ceiling, not a discovery: rare words get chopped into pieces precisely so the vocabulary stops growing. Three words of memory: a hundred quintillion cells, twenty Earths of paper.

Now the bit that matters, and take your time. The gold speck is not our room any more. It's *everything the model ever read* --- fifteen trillion tokens, the entire training run --- and it has vanished.

So do the division out loud. Read fifteen trillion tokens, fill at most fifteen trillion cells, out of a hundred quintillion. One cell in seven million. Every other row stays empty forever.

Then the callback, and this is the moment. Our own grid, three slides back, was already 98 per cent empty --- about a thousand cells with a tally in them out of 57,600. Every blank one is a pair the book never happened to make, and the model calls those impossible. That's the *easy* case: one word of memory, a picture book, and a room that holds every pair in it.

Three words in, it isn't 98 per cent empty. It's empty except for one cell in seven million, and no amount of reading will fix it. There isn't enough text in the world.
*/}

{/* _class: impact */}

a trillion numbers, and

**not one of them is a table**

{/* notes:
The resolution, and it should land as relief rather than one more big number.

Nobody builds the grid, because nobody can. Instead: a fixed pile of numbers that *works out* the row when it needs it, from the context in front of it.

Two things follow. It's smaller --- a table that looks back just two words needs a thousand times more numbers than Claude has. The maths goes the other way from how it's sold to you. And it's fixed: a longer conversation doesn't make the model bigger. The table would have squared.

That's also where "it can say what it never saw" came from, two slides ago. Because it works the row out instead of looking it up, it can fill in a cell nobody ever tallied. Generalisation isn't a bonus feature --- it's the only way out of the empty table.
*/}
