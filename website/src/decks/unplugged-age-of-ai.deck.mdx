## what you've just been given

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-cut-spread.avif)

a list of **token pairs** (bigrams) from _The Cat in the Hat_

(it's shuffled, so your page isn't just a book excerpt)

```notes
Hold one up. Each pair is a word with the word that came just before it boxed in front: `the` then `cat`, meaning that somewhere in the book, `cat` follows `the`. Every word keeps the same colour wherever it appears, so you scan by colour and confirm by reading.

"Token pair" is the wording printed on their sheet. One clause is enough for now: a token is one word or one punctuation mark. Slide 11 covers the full mapping.

In book order these pages would just be the book with boxes drawn round it, and you'd read it. Shuffled, no single page carries a story.

About 80 pairs each. Give them ten seconds to look, then get their eyes back up.
```

can I have a **volunteer**?

```notes
Someone near the front, sheet in hand. Ask them to read out any one pair from their page: both words, boxed one first.

Press `b` for the whiteboard and write both words up. Taking the seed off a sheet means the room supplies the first two words as well as every word after them.

Then explain what happens next: the second of those two words is the one I'm about to call out. The algorithm slide lands better with a real word already on the board.

If their pair is an odd one (a full stop, or a name that appears once), take it anyway; a thin first round is a real property of the model. It can't dead-end on you, so you never need a second volunteer for that reason.
```

## the algorithm

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-algorithm-generation.avif)

1. **call out** the last word we wrote (w/colour)
2. **hands up** if it appears as the _boxed_ word on your sheet
3. **pick a hand at random** and you read out the corresponding _next_ (non-boxed) word
4. **write it down**---and then repeat the process

```notes
Walk them through it once slowly against the two words already on the board, because the demo depends on step 2 and step 3.

Step 2: one hand each. If you've got more than one match, go with the first one you spot.

Step 3: I pick at random, rather than taking whoever shouts first (which would sample the fastest reader in the room instead of the book). Picking at random makes the room behave like the model.
```

# let's run it

![bg](./assets/bg-div-lessons.avif)

<Timer minutes={5} overlay />

```notes
Whiteboard is already up with the volunteer's two words. Call out the second of them and go. Five minutes on the timer, so keep the rounds moving without commentary between hands going up and a word landing on the board.

If you need to restart, the loud seeds are `the` (97 pairs out in the room across 30 different next words), then `and` (69), `I` (59), `you` (34), `we` (26). Avoid restarting from a full stop; it works, but the first thing the room does is argue about punctuation.

Two things to narrate while it runs, once each, without stopping the flow:

The hands are the distribution. Say `the` and a forest goes up; say a rare word and you get two. The number of hands for a continuation is how often it happens in the book, so the room performs weighted sampling automatically. (If someone asks: for contexts more common than the room is big, somebody holds two matches and raises one hand, which flattens the busiest contexts slightly.)

The choosing is a coin toss. When five hands are up, the four I don't pick were all real continuations of the book. A different pick gives a different sentence, for the same reason the "regenerate" button gives a different answer.

Every distinct token in this book appears as a boxed word somewhere, so every word you call has at least one continuation.

The one way the room goes quiet is an empty seat: that person's sheet went home with them, and the pairs on it are gone. If that happens, point it out as slide 6 in action and reseed.

Stop while they still want more, around 15 words. Read the sentence back.
```

## what role did you play?

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-core-insight-portrait.avif)

each word on your sheet → a **token**

your sheets → the **parameters**/**weights** of the **model**

the hands in the air → the **probability distribution**

the random pick → **sampling**

the sentence on the board → **generation**

```notes
You now have a physical referent for each piece of the standard terminology. Go down the list at a fair clip to make the mapping clear.

They have already met "token" on the sheet and back on slide 6; here it sticks, because everything else is measured in tokens. A token is one unit of text: for us, one word or one punctuation mark, which is why the full stops get picked like everything else. (A production model chops rarer words into sub-word pieces, which is how it spells out a word it has never seen. Mention this only if it comes up.) Parameters and weights are two names for the same thing.

When a researcher says a model has 2.8 trillion parameters, they mean a very large pile of the thing on your lap.
```

## three things Claude\* does that we didn't

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-attention.avif)

it looks at **more than one word** of context

it can say what it **never saw**---`cat` teaches it about `dog`

it's been taught to **answer**, not just continue

```notes
Explain the asterisk in one line: Claude here stands for any frontier model (ChatGPT, Gemini, Llama).

Give each point a single sentence; each one has a full lesson on the website.

One word of context is why our sentence wandered: when I called `the`, nobody could tell whether we'd just written "sat in the" or "hit the". Claude looks back over the whole conversation.

Our model treats any pair it never saw as impossible. A neural model spreads what it learns across billions of numbers instead of separate cells, so what it learns about `cat` carries over to `dog`.

And ours only ever continues text. Ask it a question and it continues the question, because continuing text is all it does. Turning a base model into something that answers questions takes another round of training on conversations.

The core loop stays the same: look at the context, weigh the options, pick one, write it down.
```

```notes
Three independent quantities scale up. The first is how much context the model looks at before each guess. Ours looked at one token, which is why our sentence wandered; Claude looks back over up to a million tokens.

The second is how much text went past the counter. Ours read 1,962 tokens; Thinking Machines trained its open-weights Inkling model (July 2026) on forty-five trillion.

The third is how many learned numbers the model contains. Ours had a 240 by 240 grid: 57,600 places a count could live. The largest open-weights model, Moonshot's Kimi K3 (July 2026), has 2.8 trillion parameters; the closed labs don't publish theirs.

The bars have separate scales because these quantities can be changed independently, and the endpoints are different kinds of model: grid cells on the left, neural-network parameters on the right.
```
