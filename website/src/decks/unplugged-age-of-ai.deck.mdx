# Unplugged in the age of AI

## What a room full of people can know about a machine

![bg](./assets/bg-title-wide.avif)

{/* notes:
Twenty minutes, and most of it is you doing the work rather than me talking. Introduce yourself briefly --- School of Cybernetics, and the last two years spent teaching people how language models work with paper and dice.

Ask them not to touch the sheet on their chair yet. It gets read too early otherwise, and the reveal is worth protecting.
*/}

---

{/* _class: quote */}

“Everyday life is increasingly regulated by complex technologies that most
people neither understand nor believe they can do much to influence.”

Albert Bandura, 2001

{/* notes:
Tim had this on screen about fifteen minutes ago. I want to pick it up and point it at one technology in particular, because Bandura wrote it in 2001 and it has aged into a description of this year.

Read it out. Don't gloss it yet --- the next slide does that.
*/}

---

{/* _class: impact */}

**nor believe they can do much to influence**

{/* notes:
Two failures in that sentence, and they're not equal. Not understanding a technology is ordinary --- I don't understand my car's transmission and I get by. The second clause is the expensive one: having concluded, in advance, that this is not a thing you get a say in.

That's the failure AI is currently manufacturing at scale, and it's the one this talk is aimed at.
*/}

## What we get told about AI

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-impact.avif)

it **understands** you---it's basically a mind

it's **just autocomplete**---it's basically a parlour trick

{/* notes:
Both of these are on offer everywhere, usually from people who are confident. And notice they're opposites that do the same job: each one tells you there's nothing in the middle worth your attention. Either it's beyond you, or it's beneath you. Either way, don't look inside.

Beat. The honest answer is in between and it's more interesting than both, and --- this is the bit people don't believe --- it fits in a room like this one.
*/}

---

{/* _class: impact */}

you can't have a view about a machine you've never seen the inside of

{/* notes:
This is the whole argument of the talk, stated once, before the demonstration that earns it.

Then: so let's look inside. Not a diagram of the inside --- the inside. Pick up your sheet.
*/}

## What's on your chair

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-cut-spread.avif)

a page of **entries** from _The Cat in the Hat_

each entry is a **word**, and the **word that came just before it**

shuffled, so your page isn't the book

{/* notes:
Hold one up. An entry is a next word with its context boxed in front of it: `the` then `cat`, meaning that somewhere in the book, `cat` follows `the`. Every word keeps the same colour wherever it appears, so you scan by colour and confirm by reading.

The shuffle matters, and it's worth naming: in book order these pages would just be the book with boxes drawn round it, and you'd read it. Shuffled, no page says anything. Nobody here is holding a story.

~80 entries each. Give them ten seconds to look, then get their eyes back up.
*/}

---

## Between us, we have the whole book

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-participants.avif)

**1,962** words · **240** distinct · **1,961** entries dealt round the room

no single sheet can continue the text on its own

{/* notes:
Every one of the 1,961 word-pairs in the book was dealt to exactly one person, and the deal deliberately spreads each context across as many people as it will go. So the model isn't in my laptop and it isn't on anyone's sheet --- it only exists across the whole room. If somebody had stayed home, part of it is missing and some contexts will draw no hands at all.

That's not a metaphor for how a model is distributed. For the next seven minutes it's literally where the model is.
*/}

---

## One round

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-algorithm-generation.avif)

1. I **call out** the last word we wrote
2. **hands up** if it's the boxed word on one of your entries
3. I **pick a hand at random** and you read out your next word
4. we **write it down**---and that word starts the next round

{/* notes:
Walk them through it once slowly, because the whole demo depends on step 2 and step 3.

Step 2: one hand each. If you've got more than one match, go with the first one you spot.

Step 3 is the one to labour, briefly. I pick at random --- not whoever shouts first, because that samples the fastest reader in the room rather than the book. Randomly is what makes the room behave like the model.

Don't explain why yet. They'll see it.
*/}

---

## Let's run it

<Timer minutes={6} overlay />

{/* notes:
Press `b` for the whiteboard and write the text as it comes. Seed from `the` --- it has 97 entries out in the room across 30 different next words, so the first round puts a lot of hands up and the demo starts loud.

Safe seeds if you need to restart: `and` (69 entries), `I` (59), `you` (34), `we` (26). Avoid seeding from a full stop; it works, but the first thing the room does is argue about punctuation.

Two things to narrate while it runs, once each, without stopping the flow:

The hands are the distribution. Say `the` and a forest goes up; say a rare word and you get two. Nobody in this room has been taught weighted sampling and the room is doing it anyway --- the number of hands for a continuation is how often it happens in the book. (Pedant's footnote, only if a pedant asks: for contexts more common than the room is big, somebody holds two matches and raises one hand, which flattens the busiest contexts slightly.)

The choosing is a coin toss. When five hands are up, the four I don't pick were all real continuations of the book. Different pick, different sentence. This is the same reason the "regenerate" button gives you a different answer.

If no hands go up, you've hit a context that only ever occurred at the very end of the book. Say so --- it's a nice moment, not a failure --- and reseed.

Stop while they still want more, around 15--20 words. Read the sentence back.
*/}

## What we each were

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-core-insight-portrait.avif)

your sheets → the **parameters**

the hands in the air → the **probability distribution**

my random pick → **sampling**

the sentence on the board → **generation**

{/* notes:
Four words that get used to make this stuff sound unreachable, and you've now got a physical referent for each. Go along the list at a fair clip --- the point is the mapping, not a definition of each term.

Worth saying out loud: those are the real words. When a researcher says a model has 1.5 trillion parameters, they mean this, a very large pile of the thing on your lap.
*/}

## The same model, drawn as a grid

<StaticGrid tokens={EXAMPLE_TOKENS} vocabulary={EXAMPLE_VOCAB} step={9} />

{/* notes:
A much smaller book --- "Run, Spot, run. See Spot run." --- so the whole model fits on a slide. Every entry from your sheets is a tally mark in a cell: row is the word we're on, column is the word that could come next.

This is the form we use in the workshop, where each table builds their own grid from scratch. Same model, small enough to hold in one pair of hands. Point at a cell with two marks and say: that's two hands going up.
*/}

---

{/* _class: impact */}

the sentence we wrote is **not in the book**

{/* notes:
Check it if you like --- unless we got very unlucky, that exact sentence isn't in The Cat in the Hat. Nobody wrote it. There was no lookup and no database of sentences. It came out of a pile of paper and a series of coin tosses.

That's the whole trick, and you have now watched it happen from the inside. Everything from here is a difference of degree.
*/}

## Three things Claude does that we didn't

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-attention.avif)

it looks at **more than one word** of context

it can say what it **never saw**---`cat` teaches it about `dog`

it's been taught to **answer**, not just continue

{/* notes:
Say each in a sentence, resist explaining any of them; each one has a full lesson on the website and none of them fits in this talk.

One word of context is why our sentence wandered --- when I called `the`, nobody could tell whether we'd just written "sat in the" or "hit the". Claude looks back over the whole conversation.

Our model rates any pair it never saw as flatly impossible, however sensible. A real model spreads what it learns across billions of numbers instead of separate cells, so what it learns about `cat` reaches `dog`.

And ours only ever continues text. Ask it a question and it continues the question --- that's not it refusing, answering just isn't a thing it does. Turning that into something that answers you is another round of training, on conversations.

None of these changed the loop. Look at the context, weigh the options, pick one, write it down.
*/}

a real vocabulary: **100,000** words, **10 billion** cells, **1 km²** of paper

{/* notes:
Past this the picture gives out, so switch to measuring. A commercial model's vocabulary is around 100,000 words, and the grid for that alone is 10 billion cells --- a square kilometre of paper, near enough the whole Acton campus.

And that's still the toy version: one word of context, tally marks, none of the three fixes on the last slide. The real thing at 1.5 trillion parameters, a centimetre each, is about 150 square kilometres.

The mechanism didn't change. Only the dosage.
*/}

## Back to Bandura

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-confluence.avif)

a room full of people just did, slowly, the thing you're told needs a data
centre

{/* notes:
This is why unplugged still matters, and I'd argue it matters more now than when Tim started doing it.

The technologies Bandura was worried about in 2001 were at least visibly machines. This one talks back, and the people selling it have an interest in you believing it's either a mind or a magic trick. Both stories end with you having no view.

Twenty minutes of paper and hands in the air is the cheapest known cure for that.
*/}

---

{/* _class: impact */}

it doesn't make you an expert. it makes you **hard to bluff**.

{/* notes:
Be careful not to oversell. You can't build Claude now, and nothing here tells you whether it should be marking your students' essays.

What you can do is hear a claim about AI and have somewhere to stand --- you know what kind of thing it is, which claims need more than confidence behind them, and which questions are worth asking. For a technology being sold this hard, that's not a small thing to hand a room.

And it's portable: everything here is free, printable, and needs no devices.
*/}
