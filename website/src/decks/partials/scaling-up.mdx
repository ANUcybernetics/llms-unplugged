# Scaling up

{/* notes:

The three are all the same move --- take something their grid has a little of and turn it up: how much it looks at, how much it read, how many numbers it holds. {/* notes: Ask before you explain: hands up if you've told one of these things not to do something and it did it anyway. Take two answers, then say you're about to show them the pen-and-paper version of it. The italic line is theirs, not yours --- it should feel like you're picking up something they said.

First knob, and it's sitting right there in the worked example. We're on a comma with two options and no way to choose between them, so we roll. Look at why we're stuck: the model is looking at a comma, and every comma in the text is identical to it. It cannot tell whether it just wrote `run` or `spot`. That's not bad luck, it's the design --- one word of context is all it gets. Their instruction, if they'd given one, is simply not in the room.

Keep the bridge honest. A frontier model doesn't have a one-word window, so it isn't that your instruction fell off the end. It's that the instruction is one more stretch of text competing with everything else in the window, weighted rather than obeyed --- closer to "it read your instruction and something else won" than to "it never saw it". The shared point is the one the grid makes plainly: what the model is looking at right now is all that shapes what comes next.

Then the last line, which is the one that sets up the rest of the section. Somebody always asks "why not just look at two words?" --- and you want that question. The answer is that a grid charges you for it, and the price is the whole vocabulary again: one row per *pair* of words rather than per word. Two words is a trigram, you can absolutely build one, and there's a lesson on the website that runs one with pen and paper.

Don't put a number on it yet. The next slide draws it, and the picture does more than the arithmetic. */}

{/* notes: Second knob: read more. Everyone assumes this is the whole story, and it's the one place in the section where the honest answer is genuinely surprising.

This is Frankenstein at true scale --- in the 2-hour run it's the booklet they just generated from, so name it as that; otherwise one clause is enough, a whole novel, about 84,000 words. Seven thousand distinct words, so 49 million cells. The gold speck is every word in the book. Read the entire novel and you have filled 41,000 cells: 0.08% of the table, one in twelve hundred. The rest are empty, and empty means impossible.

Now the surprise, and it's measured, not rhetorical --- it's their own tool, run on the first 5% of the novel and then on all of it. Twenty times as much text, and the grid did not get fuller. It got emptier: 0.20% of cells filled, down to 0.08%. Say why, because it's the whole point --- new text brings new *words* as well as new pairs, and every new word adds a whole row and a whole column. The table grows by squares while the tally marks grow one at a time. That is a race a grid loses forever.

So put the two knobs together, because they're the same failure. One more word of context makes it 346 *billion* cells for this one novel --- and the whole book still only fills 72,000 of them. More context makes the table bigger; more text can't fill it. Neither knob works on a grid, and turning both at once is worse than either.

Fifteen trillion tokens is the published figure for a recent open model's training run --- about 180 million copies of this novel, and roughly 5.7 million years at the pace they just worked. The question to leave hanging: if reading more can't fill a table, what is a real model doing with all that text? */}

## More parameters

<p class="voice">"it wrote something genuinely new---how?"</p>

<ParameterGrid tokens={EXAMPLE_GENERALISE} vocabulary={EXAMPLE_GENERALISE_VOCAB} scores={EXAMPLE_GENERALISE_SCORES} highlight="dog ran" />

`the dog ran` isn't in this text, so your grid rates it **impossible**

a real model rates it **0.40**---its numbers are **shared**, not one per cell

{/* notes: Ask before you explain: has anyone had one of these produce something that genuinely surprised them --- something it couldn't have been copying? Take an example from the room if you get a good one; this slide is the answer to it, and it's also the answer to the question the last slide left hanging.

Third knob, and the one that rescues the other two. A different text this time, so set it up in one breath: three sentences, `cat` and `dog` doing exactly the same job. Then the ringed cell.

Your grid tallies `cat` followed by `ran`, but never `dog` followed by `ran` --- so that cell is empty, and empty means impossible. It will never say "the dog ran", a sentence you know is perfectly fine. Every cell is its own little island; nothing the grid learnt about `cat` reaches `dog`. That islanding is exactly why reading more didn't help: every tally mark you make pays for one cell and no other.

A transformer doesn't give each pattern its own cell. It spreads every pattern across billions of numbers, each nudged a tiny amount after every wrong guess in training, and because `cat` and `dog` turn up in the same kinds of places they end up sharing numbers. One sentence about a cat now pays for a sentence about a dog. So the model can fill a cell it never saw.

This is the line to land hard, and it's the guard against everything they'll hear later: a parameter is not a cell. About a trillion parameters is the right order of magnitude for a frontier model (the exact figure for the closed ones is unpublished, so "about that, and nobody outside will tell you" is the honest answer) --- and that is not a trillion-cell grid, which we've just seen would be empty. Numbers being shared instead of separate is what turns the other two knobs from useless into worth having, and it's the reason an LLM isn't a very large phrasebook. It says things nobody ever wrote.

Two asides if they come up. These numbers are illustrative, not measured --- the shape is the point. And the cost of the fix: there's no longer a cell to point at, so "where does it store *the dog ran*" has no better answer than "smeared across a few billion numbers, and we're still working on reading it back". That's most of what interpretability research is. */}

{/* notes: The three knobs are done. This one is deliberately not a fourth --- it isn't about *how much*, it's about *which text*, and it's the difference that explains the behaviour they meet every day. Say that as you arrive: "and one thing that isn't a matter of size at all".

Ask before you explain: who's had one of these cave the moment you pushed back --- agreed with you when you were the one who had it wrong? It gets a laugh and everyone's had it. Then: to see where that habit comes from, we have to look at the one thing your grid can't do at all.

No heading --- the line is "let's ask your grid a question", and the slide just shows what comes back. Feed their model a question and it does what it always does: continues. It isn't being unhelpful and it isn't broken; nothing in "tally which word follows which" has any notion of a question and an answer. This is the honest state of a model that has only ever been trained to continue text --- and it's also, near enough, what a frontier model looks like straight out of pre-training: their problem, with better grammar. Turn all three knobs to the maximum and you still get this.

Then the second line. What turns it into something that answers you is post-training: a second, much smaller round of the same training on transcripts of conversations, plus feedback on which answers people preferred. Tally text off the web and what follows a question mark is usually another question; tally conversations and what follows it is the start of an answer.

The mechanism hasn't changed --- same predict-the-next-word loop, same dice --- just different text to learn from, so different habits on top. Don't labour it; the joke has already made the point.

Now close the loop on the question you opened with. "Feedback on which answers people preferred" is where the caving comes from: raters, faced with two replies, tended to prefer the agreeable one, so agreeing got reinforced. There is nowhere in any of this that a fact is marked true or false --- no setting, no lookup, nothing like the tally marks they can point at. "You're absolutely right" is a habit that scored well, produced by the same next-token loop as everything else. Which is also the useful takeaway: agreement from one of these is not evidence you were right. */}
