your grid sees **one word**; Claude sees **a million tokens**---the whole

{/* notes: Ask before you explain: hands up if you've told one of these things not to do something and it did it anyway. Take two answers, then say you're about to show them the pen-and-paper version of it. The italic line is theirs, not yours --- it should feel like you're picking up something they said.

First difference, and it's sitting right there in the worked example. We're on a comma with two options and no way to choose between them, so we roll. Look at why we're stuck: the model is looking at a comma, and every comma in the text is identical to it. It cannot tell whether it just wrote `run` or `spot`. That's not bad luck, it's the design --- one word of context is all it gets. Their instruction, if they'd given one, is simply not in the room.

Keep the bridge honest. A frontier model doesn't have a one-word window, so it isn't that your instruction fell off the end. It's that the instruction is one more stretch of text competing with everything else in the window, weighted rather than obeyed --- closer to "it read your instruction and something else won" than to "it never saw it". The shared point is the one the grid makes plainly: what the model is looking at right now is all that shapes what comes next.

A frontier model looks back over everything it can see, and that's the whole conversation plus whatever you pasted into it.

Someone will ask the obvious "why not just look at two words?" --- and you want that question, because the scale section at the end of this part is the answer to it in full. So bank it, don't spend it. Two words is a trigram, you can absolutely build one, and there's a lesson on the website that runs one with pen and paper. Then: "hold that thought --- what it costs you is the last thing we'll look at." */}

## It can say what it never saw

<p class="voice">"it wrote something genuinely new---how?"</p>

<ParameterGrid tokens={EXAMPLE_GENERALISE} vocabulary={EXAMPLE_GENERALISE_VOCAB} scores={EXAMPLE_GENERALISE_SCORES} highlight="dog ran" />

`the dog ran` isn't in this text, so your grid rates it **impossible**

a real model rates it **0.40**, because what it learnt about `cat` carried
over to `dog`

{/* notes: Ask before you explain: has anyone had one of these produce something that genuinely surprised them --- something it couldn't have been copying? Take an example from the room if you get a good one; this slide is the answer to it.

Second difference. A different text this time, so set it up in one breath: three sentences, `cat` and `dog` doing exactly the same job. Then the ringed cell.

Your grid tallies `cat` followed by `ran`, but never `dog` followed by `ran` --- so that cell is empty, and empty means impossible. It will never say "the dog ran", a sentence you know is perfectly fine. Every cell is its own little island; nothing the grid learnt about `cat` reaches `dog`.

A transformer doesn't give each pattern its own cell. It spreads every pattern across billions of numbers, each nudged a tiny amount after every wrong guess in training, and because `cat` and `dog` turn up in the same kinds of places they end up sharing numbers. So the model can fill a cell it never saw. That's generalisation, and it's the reason an LLM isn't a very large phrasebook --- it says things nobody ever wrote.

Two asides if they come up. These numbers are illustrative, not measured --- the shape is the point. And the cost of the fix: there's no longer a cell to point at, so "where does it store *the dog ran*" has no better answer than "smeared across a few billion numbers, and we're still working on reading it back". That's most of what interpretability research is. */}

{/* notes: Ask before you explain: who's had one of these cave the moment you pushed back --- agreed with you when you were the one who had it wrong? It gets a laugh and everyone's had it. Then: to see where that habit comes from, we have to look at the one thing your grid can't do at all.

No heading --- the line is "let's ask your grid a question", and the slide just shows what comes back. Third difference, and the funniest one. Feed their model a question and it does what it always does: continues. It isn't being unhelpful and it isn't broken; nothing in "tally which word follows which" has any notion of a question and an answer. This is the honest state of a model that has only ever been trained to continue text --- and it's also, near enough, what a frontier model looks like straight out of pre-training: their problem, with better grammar.

Then the second line. What turns it into something that answers you is post-training: a second, much smaller round of the same training on transcripts of conversations, plus feedback on which answers people preferred. Tally text off the web and what follows a question mark is usually another question; tally conversations and what follows it is the start of an answer.

The mechanism hasn't changed --- same predict-the-next-word loop, same dice --- just different text to learn from, so different habits on top. Don't labour it; the joke has already made the point.

Now close the loop on the question you opened with. "Feedback on which answers people preferred" is where the caving comes from: raters, faced with two replies, tended to prefer the agreeable one, so agreeing got reinforced. There is nowhere in any of this that a fact is marked true or false --- no setting, no lookup, nothing like the tally marks they can point at. "You're absolutely right" is a habit that scored well, produced by the same next-token loop as everything else. Which is also the useful takeaway: agreement from one of these is not evidence you were right. */}

## Three things are different

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-attention.avif)

- it sees **more than one word**
- it can say what it **never saw**
- it's been taught to **answer**, not just continue

{/* notes: Land the three, then say what's left. None of them changed the loop: look at the context, weigh the options, roll, write it down, repeat.

What's left is size, and it's worth flagging that it's not one question but two --- how much the thing read, and how much of the thing there is. People's intuition has no grip on either, and the second one has a surprise in it. */}

# Scale is a hell of a drug

![bg](./assets/bg-distribution.avif)

{/* notes: Two quantities get called "scale", and they behave completely differently. How much text went past the counter, and how big the table would have to be. Do them in that order --- the first is the one people's intuition half-copes with, and it's the setup for the second.

Keep your eye on the gold speck over the next few slides: that's theirs, drawn at true scale, on every one of them.

The unit throughout is the booklet's: one centimetre per square. Say it once here and never re-explain it. */}

---

{/* _class: impact */}

more text makes the grid **emptier**

{/* notes: Before the second zoom, the fact that makes it inevitable --- and it's in their own two texts, so it's checkable.

The Cat in the Hat: 240 distinct tokens, 57,600 cells, and 1,037 of them have a tally in them. Just under two per cent full.

Frankenstein: 7,023 distinct tokens, 49 million cells, 41,000 with a tally. Less than one cell in a thousand.

Fifty times more text, and the grid went from two per cent full to a tenth of a per cent. That's not a fluke of these two books. More text brings more distinct words, the grid grows with the *square* of those, and the tallies only grow with the text. The table always wins.

Sit on it for a second: reading more does not fill the table in. It empties it. */}

{/* notes: Second quantity, and the same book: not how long it is, but how big a table you'd need to hold it. This is where the honest bad news is.

Their grid was 25 cells: five centimetres square, smaller than a beer coaster. Frankenstein with one word of context has 7,023 rows and 7,023 next-token columns --- 49 million cells, about half a hectare. {/* notes: Take a real vocabulary --- about 100,000 tokens, and that number is a deliberate ceiling, not a discovery: the tokeniser chops rare words into pieces precisely so the vocabulary stops growing. Three words of context still produces a hundred quintillion possible cells. Twenty Earths of paper.

Now the collision, and it's the point of the whole section. The gold cell stands for everything the model could have observed directly, exaggerated enough to remain visible.

So do the division out loud. Read fifteen trillion tokens, fill at most fifteen trillion cells, out of a hundred quintillion. One cell in seven million. Every other row stays empty forever, and an empty row means "impossible" --- the same empty cell that made `the dog ran` unsayable a few slides ago, but now covering essentially the entire table.

That's the real reason nobody builds this. Not that it's too big to print. It's too big to ever fill --- there isn't enough text in the world, and there never will be. And this is still only a three-word memory. */}

{/* _class: impact */}

so it **isn't a table**

**~1 trillion** numbers that work it out instead

{/* notes: The resolution, and it lands as relief rather than another big number.

A real model doesn't store a row per context, because it can't. It keeps a fixed pile of numbers --- about a trillion, and that figure is unpublished for the closed models, so "about that, and nobody outside will tell you" is the honest answer --- and *computes* the row it needs, on demand, from the context in front of it.

Two things follow, and they're the ones worth carrying out of the room.

It's smaller. A trillion sounds vast, but a table with just two words of context over a real vocabulary needs a thousand times more numbers than that --- and the model has a million tokens of context, not two. The neural network is a *compression* of the grid, not an inflation of it.

And it's fixed. Doubling the length of your conversation does not double the model. The table would have squared.

That's also where the second difference came from, an hour ago now: because it works the row out rather than looking it up, it can fill in a cell it never saw. Generalisation isn't a bonus feature bolted on. It's the only way out of the empty table.

The mechanism didn't change. What changed is that it stopped storing the answers.
*/}
