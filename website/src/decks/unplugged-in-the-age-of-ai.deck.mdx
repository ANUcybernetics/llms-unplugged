# LLMs Unplugged

## Does 'Unplugged' still matter in the age of AI?

![bg](./assets/bg-title-wide.avif)

```notes
Twenty minutes, and most of it is you doing the work. Introduce yourself briefly, and mention that you've spent the last couple of years teaching people how language models work with paper and dice.

Hold the sheets back until slide 4 so the room doesn't read ahead.
```

{/* prettier-ignore */}
> Everyday life is increasingly regulated by complex technologies that most
> people <span class="fragment quote-emph-minor">neither understand</span>
> <span class="fragment quote-emph">nor believe they can do much to
> influence</span>.
>
> <cite>Albert Bandura, 2001</cite>

```notes
Bandura wrote this in 2001, about technology in general, and it has aged into a description of this year. I want to point it at one technology in particular.

Read it out whole first, with nothing lit up. Then two clicks for the two distinct failures in that sentence.

First click, `neither understand`: ordinary. I don't understand my car's transmission and I get by.

Second click, `nor believe they can do much to influence`: the expensive one, where people conclude in advance that they get no say in it. AI is currently manufacturing that failure at scale, and this talk is aimed at it.
```

## what we get told about AI

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-dialogue.avif)

it **understands** you---it's basically a mind

it's **just autocomplete**---it's basically a parlour trick

```notes
Both of these are on offer everywhere, usually from people who sound confident. Notice that they are opposites doing the same job: each one tells you there's nothing in the middle worth your attention. Either it's beyond you, or it's beneath you. Either way, don't look inside.

The reality sits between the two, and it fits in a room like this one.
```

## between us, we have the whole book

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-participants.avif)

**1,962** words · **240** distinct · **1,961** token pairs dealt round the room

no single sheet has the whole picture

```notes
Every one of the 1,961 word-pairs in the book was dealt to one person, and the deal spreads each context across as many people as possible. For the last five minutes, the model existed across the whole room. If somebody had stayed home, part of it would be missing and some contexts would draw no hands at all.
```

```notes
One line out of the book you're all holding ("Look at me! Look at me! Look at me now!"), small enough that the whole model fits on a slide. Every pair from your sheets is a tally mark in a cell: the row is the token we're on, and the column is the token that could come next.

Point at the `me` row: two marks under `!`, one under `now`. That corresponds to three hands going up, two of them holding `!`: the same distribution you were making with your arms five minutes ago.

We use this grid form in the workshop, where each table builds their own grid from scratch.
```

## three things Claude\* does that we didn't

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-attention.avif)

it looks at **more than one word** of context

it can say what it **never saw**---`cat` teaches it about `dog`

it's been taught to **answer**, not just continue

```notes
Explain the asterisk in one line: Claude here stands for any frontier model (ChatGPT, Gemini, Llama).

Give each point a single sentence; each one has a full module on the website.

One word of context is why our sentence wandered: when I called `the`, nobody could tell whether we'd just written "sat in the" or "hit the". Claude looks back over the whole conversation.

Our model treats any pair it never saw as impossible. A neural model spreads what it learns across billions of numbers instead of separate cells, so what it learns about `cat` carries over to `dog`.

And ours only ever continues text. Ask it a question and it continues the question, because continuing text is all it does. Turning a base model into something that answers questions takes another round of training on conversations.

The core loop stays the same: look at the context, weigh the options, pick one, write it down.
```

```notes
Three independent dials separate our room from a frontier LLM. First, context window: how much text the model sees before each guess. We looked one token back; Claude looks back up to a million tokens.

Second, training data: how many tokens went past the counter. Ours saw 1,962 tokens; Thinking Machines' open-weights Inkling model (July 2026) trained on forty-five trillion.

Third, model size: we held 1,961 token pairs on paper, where Claude Fable 5 is estimated at about five trillion learned parameters (Anthropic doesn't publish the figure).

Each bar has its own scale because the three dials move independently, and the endpoints are different architectures: lookup counts on the left, neural network weights on the right.
```

## back to Bandura

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-confluence.avif)

> most people neither understand nor **believe they can do much to influence**

a room full of people just did, slowly, the thing you're told needs a data
centre

```notes
Read the clause off the slide again: it's the one we lit up at the start, and the one the last twenty minutes were aimed at.

This is why unplugged activities matter even more now than when CS Unplugged started.

The technologies Bandura was worried about in 2001 were at least visibly machines. This one talks back, and the people selling it have an interest in you believing it's either a mind or a magic trick. Both stories leave you with no view of your own.

Twenty minutes of paper and hands in the air is the quickest cure for that.
```
