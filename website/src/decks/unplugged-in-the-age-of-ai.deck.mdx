# LLMs Unplugged

## Does 'Unplugged' still matter in the age of AI?

![bg](./assets/bg-title-wide.avif)

```notes
Twenty minutes, and most of it is you doing the work rather than me talking. Introduce yourself briefly --- and that you've spent the last couple of years teaching people how language models work with paper and dice.

Don't hand out the sheets yet. They get read too early otherwise, and the reveal is worth protecting.
```

{/* _class: quote */}

![bg](./assets/bg-bandura-quote.avif)

{/* prettier-ignore */}
> Everyday life is increasingly regulated by complex technologies that most
> people <span class="fragment quote-emph-minor">neither understand</span>
> <span class="fragment quote-emph">nor believe they can do much to
> influence</span>.
>
> <cite>Albert Bandura, 2001</cite>

```notes
Bandura wrote this in 2001, about technology in general, and it has aged into a description of this year. I want to point it at one technology in particular.

Read it out whole first, with nothing lit up. Then two clicks, because there are two failures in that sentence and they are not equal.

First click, `neither understand`: ordinary. I don't understand my car's transmission and I get by.

Second click, `nor believe they can do much to influence`: the expensive one. Having concluded, in advance, that this is not a thing you get a say in. That's the failure AI is currently manufacturing at scale, and it's the one this talk is aimed at.
```

## what we get told about AI

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-dialogue.avif)

it **understands** you---it's basically a mind

it's **just autocomplete**---it's basically a parlour trick

```notes
Both of these are on offer everywhere, usually from people who are confident. And notice they're opposites that do the same job: each one tells you there's nothing in the middle worth your attention. Either it's beyond you, or it's beneath you. Either way, don't look inside.

Beat. The honest answer is in between and it's more interesting than both, and --- this is the bit people don't believe --- it fits in a room like this one.
```

## between us, we have the whole book

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-participants.avif)

**1,962** words · **240** distinct · **1,961** token pairs dealt round the room

no single sheet has the whole picture

```notes
Every one of the 1,961 word-pairs in the book was dealt to exactly one person, and the deal deliberately spreads each context across as many people as it will go. So the model wasn't in my laptop and it wasn't on anyone's sheet --- for the last five minutes it existed only across the whole room. If somebody had stayed home, part of it would be missing and some contexts would draw no hands at all.

That's not a metaphor for how a model is distributed. It's literally where the model was.
```

## three things Claude\* does that we didn't

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-attention.avif)

it looks at **more than one word** of context

it can say what it **never saw**---`cat` teaches it about `dog`

it's been taught to **answer**, not just continue

```notes
The asterisk is yours to explain, in one line: Claude here means any frontier model --- ChatGPT, Gemini, Llama. Nothing on this slide is specific to Anthropic's; I just need a name to point at.

Say each in a sentence, resist explaining any of them; each one has a full module on the website and none of them fits in this talk.

One word of context is why our sentence wandered --- when I called `the`, nobody could tell whether we'd just written "sat in the" or "hit the". Claude looks back over the whole conversation.

Our model rates any pair it never saw as flatly impossible, however sensible. A real model spreads what it learns across billions of numbers instead of separate cells, so what it learns about `cat` reaches `dog`.

And ours only ever continues text. Ask it a question and it continues the question --- that's not it refusing, answering just isn't a thing it does. Turning that into something that answers you is another round of training, on conversations.

None of these changed the loop. Look at the context, weigh the options, pick one, write it down.
```

{/* _class: centered */}

<ModelScaleBars
  contextStart="1 token"
  trainingStart="1,962 tokens"
  modelStart="57,600 grid cells"
  footer="three independent scales · different kinds of model"
/>

```notes
Three independent scales grow. The first is how much the model looks at before each guess. Ours looked at exactly one token --- which is why our sentence wandered --- and Claude looks back over the whole conversation.

The second is how much text went past the counter. Ours read 1,962 tokens; fifteen trillion is the published figure for a recent open model's training run. Every one of those tokens went past a counter, the way the book went past yours.

The third is how many learned numbers the model contains. Ours had a 240 by 240 grid: 57,600 places a count could live. A trillion parameters is the right order of magnitude for a frontier model, and the honest answer for the closed ones is "about that, and nobody outside will tell you".

The bars have separate scales because these quantities can be changed independently. And the endpoints are different kinds of model: grid cells on the left, neural-network parameters on the right. This is a scale comparison, not a claim that one is a compressed version of the other.
```

**much more** context

**much more** training text

**many more** learned numbers

a **different kind** of model

```notes
Those are the differences to carry forward. Context, training text and learned numbers are all differences of scale, and none of them determines the others. What makes them worth scaling is the last line: spreading what it learns between related words --- and looking usefully across a whole conversation --- comes from different machinery, not merely from making our grid enormous.

No transformer lesson today. The important continuity is on the next slide.
```

## back to Bandura

![bg right:40%](./assets/bg-confluence.avif)

> most people neither understand nor **believe they can do much to influence**

a room full of people just did, slowly, the thing you're told needs a data
centre

```notes
Read the clause off the slide again --- it's the one we lit up at the start, and it's the one the last twenty minutes were aimed at.

This is why unplugged still matters, and I'd argue it matters more now than when CS Unplugged started.

The technologies Bandura was worried about in 2001 were at least visibly machines. This one talks back, and the people selling it have an interest in you believing it's either a mind or a magic trick. Both stories end with you having no view.

Twenty minutes of paper and hands in the air is the cheapest known cure for that.
```

it doesn't make you an expert

it makes you **harder to bullsh\*t**

```notes
Be careful not to oversell. You can't build Claude now, and nothing here tells you whether it should be marking your students' essays.

What you can do is hear a claim about AI and have somewhere to stand --- you know what kind of thing it is, which claims need more than confidence behind them, and which questions are worth asking. For a technology being sold this hard, that's not a small thing to hand a room.

And it's portable: everything here is free, printable, and needs no devices.
```
