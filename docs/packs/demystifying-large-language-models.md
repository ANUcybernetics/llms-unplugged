# Demystifying large language models --- what you need

A fifteen-minute talk for any audience, up to a few hundred. One person gives
it; the room holds the sheets and puts hands up.

## search-sheets.pdf

120 A4 sheets of token pairs, dealt from three books together. This is the
model, and it only exists spread across the room --- one sheet per seat.

Print it once for a room of 120. For a bigger hall, print the whole set again
rather than dealing more sheets: three copies covers ~360, every token pair is
then held by three people, the proportions are unchanged, and one empty seat no
longer takes a pair out of the room.

Print single-sided, at full A4. Don't reduce it two-up onto A5: the sheets are
read at arm's length, in a darkened hall, by people who have never seen one
before.

Hand them out on slide 4 and not before, fast --- "take one, pass the rest
along". Brief your ushers beforehand; the run can't start until nearly everyone
is holding one.

Nothing on these sheets names a book. That is the talk's reveal and it lands
near the end, so don't print a set with the titles on.

## lectern-brief.pdf

One page: what the sheets are and a worked example. For you, at the lectern.
Not part of the handout stack.

## slides.pdf

The deck. TED-style --- the speaking carries it and the slides are cues, so
only three of them carry real text.

## presenter-guide.pdf

Every slide followed by its own speaker-notes page. The notes are beats to hit,
not a script. Read it once before you deliver and take it to the lectern.

## Also needed, not in this pack

- ushers to hand the sheets out on cue
- a whiteboard, or the deck's annotation layer, to write the growing sentence
  where the back of the hall can read it. Write big: fifteen tokens at
  whiteboard size is the whole output
- a projector
